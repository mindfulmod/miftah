# Letter Garden Picture-Book Experience Research

## Research question

How can Letter Garden become a wordless, child-readable learning adventure that
creates wonder without distracting from Arabic letter learning?

This study treats picture books, playful learning, animated storybooks, and
children's digital games as evidence for one visual system. It does not use any
single product as a style template.

## Executive synthesis

The current prototype proves the interaction flow, but its visual grammar is
still an app grammar: floating panels, progress bars, badges, and map nodes sit
over a pleasant scene. A picture-book experience works differently. The scene
itself carries the meaning. Scale, lighting, posture, repetition, and change tell
the child what happened and what to do next.

The new Letter Garden should therefore be designed as a sequence of playable
illustrated spreads:

- A panoramic spread establishes place and invites exploration.
- A visual cue creates anticipation before the next interaction.
- A close-up spread concentrates attention on one learning moment.
- A page-turn transition provides rhythm and reveals the consequence.
- Returning to the panorama shows that the world has changed.

This gives the child an adventure arc without requiring plot, dialogue, or
readable instructions. The child is not following a written story. They are
causing a visual story to happen.

## What attracts and holds young children's attention

### 1. Children read the image before the interface

Eye-tracking studies consistently show that preschool children look at
illustrations earlier and longer than print. A recent nonlinear picture-book
study found an image-first pattern in both first fixations and total viewing
time. This supports a strict Letter Garden rule: no essential action may depend
on a label, caption, progress number, or written instruction.

Design consequence:

- The next action must be readable from composition, gesture, sound, and motion.
- Arabic letters remain the only visible language content in the child flow.
- Adult and accessibility labels can exist semantically, but not as visible
  English instructional copy.

Sources:

### 2. Wordless art makes the child a collaborator

The Eric Carle Museum describes wordless picture books as a form that asks the
reader to collaborate in storytelling. Facial expression, body language, image
scale, lighting, and color replace explanatory text. BookTrust similarly notes
that young readers notice details adults often miss and can construct their own
stories from them.

Design consequence:

- Scenes should contain interpretable visual clues rather than literal labels.
- The pet should look, point, lean, listen, and react instead of presenting
  speech bubbles with instructions.
- Each spread should contain two or three optional details that reward lingering.
- A recurring small creature or object can travel through the world as a visual
  rhyme the child learns to notice.

### 3. Wonder is built through anticipation and reveal

Picture books create momentum through the page turn. Strong examples conceal,
foreshadow, zoom, reverse scale, and reveal a surprise after a pause. Shaun Tan
describes page turns that progressively move a reader closer to a mysterious
world. The Carle Museum describes covers and endpapers as part of the narrative,
not packaging around it.

Design consequence:

- Screen changes should feel like page turns, peeks through leaves, opening
  gates, unfurling maps, or following a trail.
- Every lesson should have a small anticipation beat before the target appears.
- Completion should reveal a meaningful world change, not only stars or confetti.
- Wide vistas and intimate close-ups should alternate to create visual rhythm.

Sources:

- [Shaun Tan: Viewer Book](https://www.shauntan.net/viewer-book)

### 4. Motion should direct attention, not decorate everything

An eye-tracking study of animated storybooks found that motion can guide
children's attention to the exact visual detail that matters for comprehension.
The effect depends on motion matching the narrated concept. Other research
suggests illustrated stories offer a strong balance for preschool attention and
language networks, while excessive animation can over-direct attention.

Design consequence:

- Only one primary learning object moves assertively at a time.
- Ambient motion is slow and peripheral: drifting seeds, breathing foliage,
  blinking creatures, and changing light.
- Instructional motion is brief and specific: a pet gaze, a leaf turning, a
  letter pulsing with its sound, or a path lighting toward one destination.
- Celebration motion changes the environment first and adds particles second.

Sources:

- [Benefits of Motion in Animated Storybooks](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01591/full)
- [Functional connectivity during audio, illustrated, and animated stories](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31144523/)
- [Background elements and children's visual attention](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37757565/)

### 5. Richness should be concentrated, not uniform

Picture-book illustration can be lush without making every area equally busy.
Research documents cultural variation in object density and visual crowding,
while accessibility research shows that reducing irrelevant background elements
can help some children focus on the intended object.

Design consequence:

- Use three detail zones: quiet sky or light, story-rich landmarks, and a clear
  interaction focus.
- Reserve dense clusters for discoveries, completed areas, and page edges.
- Keep the learning target surrounded by generous visual quiet.
- Offer a low-stimulation mode that reduces ambient movement and secondary
  discoveries without removing the world.

Sources:

- [Cultural Differences in Visual Contents in Picture Books](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00304/full)

### 6. Playful learning needs agency, meaning, iteration, and joy

The LEGO Foundation's research framework describes playful learning as joyful,
meaningful, actively engaging, iterative, and socially interactive. NAEYC
describes curiosity-supporting environments as invitations to explore and try
ideas, not displays that prescribe one narrow response. Sesame Workshop stresses
child-centered, heartfelt, play-based media grounded in formative research.

Design consequence:

- The child should be able to tap harmless world details without failing.
- Wrong answers should visibly invite another attempt and keep the world warm.
- Progress should be meaningful inside the world: a bridge completes, a bird
  returns, a lantern lights, or a garden blooms.
- Optional discoveries can connect digital play to real-world curiosity about
  plants, weather, sound, animals, and pattern.

### 7. First reactions must be tested with children, not inferred by adults

PBS KIDS asks children to play unassisted and uses their first reactions to
judge whether a game is understandable. It explicitly includes children who
speak little or no English.

Design consequence:

- Every calibration scene needs a five-child first-use test before production.
- An adult should not explain where to tap.
- Record first fixation, first tap, hesitation, accidental exits, retry behavior,
  and spontaneous delight.
- If three of five children need verbal instruction, the visual cue has failed.

Source:

- [PBS KIDS Playtesting](https://www.pbs.org/parents/playtesting)

### 8. Feedback should reveal consequence without interrupting play

Apple's feedback guidance recommends clear, consistent responses that help a
person understand what changed and what can happen next. It also notes that the
delivery should match the significance of the result and that alerts disrupt
the current context. This complements the playful-learning requirement that the
child's action feel meaningful.

Design consequence:

- A completed lesson changes the illustrated world instead of opening a modal
  reward screen.
- The learned landmark blooms and the next destination wakes within one shared
  feedback window.
- The companion celebrates the child's action, then becomes calm again.
- The new world state persists after the transient motion ends.

Sources:

- [Apple Human Interface Guidelines: Feedback](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/feedback)
- [Apple Human Interface Guidelines: Design principles](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/design-principles)

### 9. Teach one action in context instead of presenting a tutorial

Apple's onboarding guidance recommends letting people understand an app or game
through experience, teaching through interactivity, and keeping any necessary
tip beside the specific task. It also warns against teaching too much at once.
That is especially relevant to a pre-literate child flow where a conventional
tutorial would introduce the reading problem the game is designed to avoid.

Design consequence:

- If the child pauses for three seconds, demonstrate only the next available
  action in its existing location.
- Assembly pieces move partway toward their sockets and return; they are never
  placed automatically.
- Choice objects lift in sequence without revealing the correct answer.
- The cue runs once, stops immediately when the child acts, and never blocks or
  dims the play surface.

Source:

- [Apple Human Interface Guidelines: Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)

### 10. Child controls need generous targets and separation

Apple recommends at least `44 × 44` points for frequently used iOS game
controls, visible press states, and enough surrounding space to reduce accidental
selection. Its accessibility guidance treats spacing as equally important to
the control's measured size. The evidence-based TIDRC framework further warns
that touchscreen interaction guidance for adults cannot simply be assumed to
fit children ages two through seven.

Design consequence:

- Letter Garden uses a stronger `56 × 56` CSS-pixel minimum for every frequent
  child-facing control at the main phone breakpoint.
- Adjacent dressing controls keep at least 10 pixels between measured boxes.
- The entire visible control is interactive and responds on touch-down; no tiny
  icon is placed inside an unrelated invisible hit rectangle.
- The prototype-only calibration rail is excluded because it does not ship in
  the child-facing game.

Sources:

- [Apple Human Interface Guidelines: Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)
- [Apple Human Interface Guidelines: Game controls](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/game-controls)
- [Touchscreen Interaction Design Recommendations for Children](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/378/2019/04/TIDRC-Framework-soni-et-al-IDC19-final.pdf)

### 11. Feedback should reinforce the idea, and the child's choices should endure

The Joan Ganz Cooney Center's review of early literacy apps applies the four
pillars of learning to digital experiences: activity should be active,
engaging, meaningful, and socially interactive. It specifically identifies
immediate, meaningful feedback as a way to sustain engagement without letting
game rewards displace the learning content. Sesame Workshop's preschool touch
guidance likewise recommends pairing dialogue with visual reinforcement for
children who cannot yet read. A broader review of early-childhood apps notes
that touch interfaces can support autonomy and agency when children can make
real choices inside the experience.

Design consequence:

- Replaying a displayed letter's Arabic sound also makes that letter respond;
  the speaker animation alone is not the teaching feedback.
- In an auditory discrimination task, the target letter remains concealed so
  the visual response never gives away the answer.
- A wrong choice produces a calm local response, repeats the learning stimulus,
  and returns control without a modal interruption.
- Companion, color, and fitted-accessory choices persist across visits. The
  character remains recognizably the child's choice instead of resetting to a
  default every session.
- Storage failure never blocks play; it only removes cross-session continuity.

Sources:

- [Getting a Read on the App Stores](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/jgcc_gettingaread.pdf)
- [Best Practices: Designing Touch Tablet Experiences for Preschoolers](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/publication/best-practices-designing-touch-tablet-experiences-for-preschoolers/)
- [Classification and evaluation of educational apps for early childhood](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9398896/)

### 12. The world should preserve a cumulative learning sequence

The What Works Clearinghouse gives strong evidence for explicitly linking
speech sounds to their written letters. Its foundational-reading guide also
recommends continued practice: introduce a new letter sound alongside a small
group of previously learned sounds and revisit them in meaningful contexts.
IES guidance on structured literacy similarly describes instruction as
sequential and cumulative, with each lesson building on prior knowledge rather
than silently discarding it.

Design consequence:

- The first unfinished letter is the single awake destination. Later landmarks
  remain asleep so the visual world expresses the learning sequence.
- Completing that lesson permanently changes it to a grown landmark and wakes
  the next prerequisite.
- Learned landmarks remain tappable for voluntary review; completion never
  removes access to earlier practice.
- Journey state survives a reload, while corrupt or unavailable storage falls
  back to the safe introductory sequence.
- Progress remains visible as changes to the landscape, not scores, streaks, or
  a dashboard layered over the story world.

Sources:

- [Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/21/Published)
- [Foundational Reading Practice Guide, Recommendation 2](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- [Putting the Science of Reading into Practice](https://ies.ed.gov/learn/blog/partner-spotlight-putting-science-reading-practice)

### 13. Scaffolding should follow the child's current step and then fade

IES early-literacy guidance describes scaffolding as the temporary feedback
that lets a child demonstrate a skill they could not yet complete alone, with
responsibility gradually returning to the child. Research observing families
using tablets identifies hints, repetition, encouragement, and pointing as
distinct supports rather than one generic help action. Sesame Workshop also
warns that text-based help is not useful for most preschool children. Recent
adaptive-intervention research provides a useful restraint: use minimal,
non-intrusive prompts and cap their repetition instead of continuously
interrupting play.

Design consequence:

- The first pause can trigger one local, wordless demonstration.
- A wrong choice or a correctly completed partial step establishes a new task
  state and may arm one new contextual cue for what remains.
- No identical state receives the same cue twice, and a lesson can show at most
  two automatic cues in total.
- A cue never completes the action, eliminates an answer, or exposes the correct
  choice; it only repeats the stimulus or demonstrates the interaction grammar.
- Correct input immediately cancels pending retry audio and hint timers so old
  feedback cannot interrupt success.

Sources:

- [IES Emergent Literacy: Print Knowledge](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/rel/regions/southeast/pdf/REL_2021045_module1_facilitator.pdf)
- [Parent Scaffolding of Young Children When Engaged with Mobile Technology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4861828/)
- [Sesame Workshop: Creating the Best Interactive Design](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/SesameWorkshop-2012.pdf)
- [Minimal, adaptive prompting in a child intervention](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13054044/)

### 14. A child must not be able to learn the answer's screen position

Large-scale assessment experiments find small but systematic effects from the
position of correct responses and distractors. Related work specifically warns
that test authors often place correct answers in middle positions, creating an
unwanted strategic clue. This evidence comes mainly from older examinees, so
applying it to a preschool letter game is a conservative design inference, not
a claim that the measured effect size transfers directly. The risk is greater
here because every old Letter Garden answer occupied the same middle stone.
Touchscreen research also shows that young children can develop repetitive
tapping behavior and that interaction unrelated to the teaching content can
interfere with learning.

Design consequence:

- Correct letters use all three positions across the ordered curriculum.
- Starting another attempt rotates the correct position, including after a
  reload, so location cannot become a durable shortcut.
- Choices stay in the same places throughout one active attempt. An error,
  sound replay, or hint never reshuffles objects under the child's hand.
- The answer state is derived from the letter identity, never from a hard-coded
  array index.
- Hints continue to animate every option equally and therefore do not leak the
  newly varied answer position.

Sources:

- [Position of Correct Option and Distractors Impacts Responses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37663536/)
- [The Position of Distractors in Multiple-Choice Test Items](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.731763/full)
- [All Tapped Out: Touchscreen Interactivity and Young Children's Word Learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388766/)

### 15. One lucky tap is not enough evidence to grow a landmark

Recent work with children ages five to six finds that retrieval practice can
improve later recall and recognition when children first receive enough initial
learning to retrieve successfully. Earlier preschool research similarly found
that cued retrieval became substantially more effective when paired with
immediate feedback. IES implementation guidance recommends frequent
opportunities to respond and immediate corrective feedback. None of these
sources specifies that a game should use exactly two response rounds; choosing
two here is the prototype's smallest low-friction confirmation of recognition.

Design consequence:

- Auditory and visual-choice lessons require two successful recognitions before
  the world records completion.
- The first success receives immediate companion and object feedback, then the
  same letter is retrieved once more with the answer in a different position.
- Errors never erase a previous success or create a streak penalty.
- Letter assembly remains a single round because placing its two separate dots
  already provides two successful, state-dependent actions.
- The confirmation transition briefly removes the choices before changing
  their order so no letter visibly jumps beneath the child's finger.

Sources:

- [Multiple Practice Success Scaffolds Long-Term Test-Enhanced Learning in Preschoolers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12598443/)
- [A Preliminary Recipe for Obtaining a Testing Effect in Preschool Children](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6110808/)
- [IES Strategies to Improve Reading implementation materials](https://ies.ed.gov/learn/blog/strategies-improve-reading-stir-implementation-materials)

### 16. Finish a chapter by fulfilling the world, not by paying the child

The Cooney Center's digital-games guide distinguishes feedback that makes the
learning consequence legible from reward systems that replace intrinsic
motivation with points. Its discussion of ST Math is especially relevant: the
mascot's visible movement through the world is part of the pedagogy rather than
a separate prize. Reviews of educational game design likewise associate clear
goals, efficacious-play feedback, player control, and room for mistakes with
more durable learning. Motivation research further supports informative,
non-controlling responses that reinforce competence, autonomy, and belonging.

Design consequence:

- Completing the sixth letter awakens one restrained final grove in the world.
- Six blossoms connect the final transformation to the six learned landmarks
  without adding a counter, badge, streak, chest, or written congratulations.
- The new canopy and warm light persist on reload, so the child's action leaves
  a durable mark rather than producing a disposable celebration overlay.
- Every completed landmark remains directly replayable after the transformation.
- Motion is concentrated into the first reveal; the fulfilled world then rests.

Sources:

- [MindShift Guide to Digital Games and Learning](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/MindShift-GuidetoDigitalGamesandLearning.pdf)
- [Educational Games and Their Current State of Play](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11268831/)
- [Feedback and Young Children's Motivation in Digital Games](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12338047/)
- [Autonomy-Supportive Robot Feedback for Children's Learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7806035/)

### 17. The companion should share attention, not become a tutorial presenter

Joint attention coordinates a learner's attention with a social partner around
the same object. Studies of contingent media and virtual agents identify gaze,
head turns, gesture, and response timing as meaningful social cues. A virtual
game study with children found that combinations of gaze, head turn, and
pointing changed how children allocated visual attention. Research with
toddlers likewise describes gaze and attention-directing social feedback as
mechanisms that support learning from screens. The exact transfer from those
settings to Letter Garden is an inference, so the cue timing must be validated
with first-use child testing rather than treated as settled fact.

Design consequence:

- A genuinely new journey begins with no falsely completed letters and only ا
  available.
- After a short, interruption-free pause, the chosen companion looks and leans
  toward ا; the landmark then lifts once in response.
- The cue contains no hand cursor, arrow, tooltip, narration, or written command.
- Any navigation or world interaction cancels the cue immediately.
- The sequence runs once per world visit while the journey is still untouched;
  it disappears permanently as soon as the first letter is completed.
- Reduced-motion mode keeps the companion's directional pose and the active
  landmark state without depending on travel animation.

Sources:

- [Building a responsive teacher: contingent gaze and virtual tutors](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4448798/)
- [The Impact of Cues on Joint Attention in Virtual Games](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11505074/)
- [Co-Viewing Supports Toddlers' Word Learning from Video](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5777334/)
- [PBS KIDS Playtesting](https://www.pbs.org/parents/playtesting)

### 18. Exploration should stay available while learning receives the salience

Research with preschool touchscreen apps distinguishes interactivity that
supports the learning task from incidental hotspots that merely solicit taps.
In one study, irrelevant hotspots competed with listening and did not reliably
direct attention, while task-aligned interaction was designed to support the
named object. A meta-analysis of technology-enhanced storybooks similarly found
that interactive features can harm comprehension when children must juggle the
story and unrelated activities. Other work emphasizes that well-crafted
interactivity can help when it draws attention to the information being learned.

Design consequence:

- Entering an unfinished orchard preserves the optional water, bird, and nook,
  but none of them receives automatic movement or emphasis.
- After a quiet pause, the companion shifts gaze toward the letter stone and
  the stone answers with one contained lift.
- Tapping the letter, a discovery, navigation, or the nook cancels the cue
  immediately; the app never fights the child's chosen focus.
- The cue does not run after lesson success, when the orchard's environmental
  transformation should own the page.
- This salience hierarchy must be tested by recording first fixation and first
  tap, not by asking adults whether the screen looks obvious.

Sources:

- [All Tapped Out: Touchscreen Interactivity and Young Children's Word Learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388766/)
- [Benefits and Pitfalls of Multimedia and Interactive Features](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204/)
- [When and How Interactive Digital Media Help Children Connect Learning](https://academic.oup.com/cdpers/article/12/3/210/8231533)
- [Best Practices: Touch Tablet Experiences for Preschoolers](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/publication/best-practices-designing-touch-tablet-experiences-for-preschoolers/)

### 19. One response must resolve before another response can change the state

Young children commonly produce repetitive taps and extremely short response
times on touchscreens, reflecting still-developing inhibitory control rather
than deliberate multi-answer input. In a preschool word-learning study, the
app was explicitly designed not to advance during object labeling regardless
of how often a child tapped. A separate early-childhood touchscreen task
physically removed the device between trials to prevent excess touches, while
also avoiding time pressure. Reading guidance from IES emphasizes frequent
opportunities to respond paired with clear, immediate corrective feedback.

Design consequence:

- A wrong choice immediately settles and the companion responds, but the other
  stones cannot accept a second result during that 760-millisecond beat.
- The sound control shares the same brief lock so overlapping replay and retry
  timers cannot create contradictory feedback.
- As soon as the object response finishes, every choice reopens and the letter
  sound repeats; the child is never placed on a countdown.
- Errors do not move the answer, erase a previous successful round, deduct
  progress, or trigger negative language.
- The lock is local to the answer group, exposed as a busy state, and cleared
  whenever the lesson is reset.

Sources:

- [All Tapped Out: Touchscreen Interactivity and Young Children's Word Learning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388766/)
- [The Early Childhood Inhibitory Touchscreen Task](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8638877/)
- [IES Strategies to Improve Reading implementation guide](https://ies.ed.gov/sites/default/files/rel-midwest/document/2026/04/STIR-ImplementGuide-508.pdf)

### 20. The voice is curriculum, so its picture must follow the real sound

Foundational-reading guidance recommends teaching a small set of letter-sound
relationships at a time, explicitly modeling each relationship, and giving the
child repeated practice. Multimedia-learning research also warns against
separating related narration and imagery in time: the child should see the
specific visual response while hearing the sound it represents. Letter Garden
therefore cannot treat the voice as decorative UI feedback or approximate its
duration with a timer.

Design consequence:

- The selected Hamed-C recordings are the primary model for all six prototype
  letters; device speech is only a fault-tolerant fallback.
- The sound bloom, target letter, and listening companion begin together and
  settle from the media's actual end event, not an assumed duration.
- Replaying first cancels the previous recording, speech fallback, timers, and
  callbacks so two pronunciation models can never overlap.
- If a browser blocks delayed playback, the sound bloom remains quietly
  ringed as a wordless replay affordance instead of pretending the child heard
  the letter.
- File mapping is explicit because Arabic near-twins are a content-integrity
  risk: ta is ت, taa is ط, haa is ح, and ha is ه.

Sources:

- [IES Foundational Skills practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/practiceGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_040717.pdf)
- [Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9762622/)
- [Temporal contiguity and novice learners](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7082416/)

### 21. The world must explain the game, not merely decorate it

Research on educational games distinguishes intrinsic integration from the
familiar “quiz inside unrelated scenery” pattern. Learning tasks are more
coherent when the action, the instructional goal, and the environment describe
the same event. Early-literacy game reports likewise warn that disconnected
mini-games can feel like interruptions even when each activity is competent in
isolation. Visual polish alone does not repair that structural seam.

Design consequence:

- Preserve the live Letter Garden's proven first chapter loop and mechanics:
  meet the Boat Letters, then Pop, Trace, and Feed. The prototype is an art and
  environment evolution, not a second curriculum.
- Give each mechanic a physical reason to exist in one connected region: sound
  tokens rise from a breezy stream meadow, tracing happens in a broad sand
  terrace under a tree, and letter fruit is offered in the orchard clearing.
- Reuse one horizon, palette, contour, paper grain, companion, audio prompt,
  and three-step trail so changing activities feels like walking within the
  same chapter rather than opening another app.
- Keep the actual learning object highest in contrast. Environmental changes
  should improve orientation and meaning without competing with the letter.
- Remove dead air inherited from the old presentation: the first selectable
  targets enter the playable view immediately while keeping the original game
  rules and adaptive behavior.

Sources:

## The picture-book principles Letter Garden should adopt

1. **The whole surface tells the story.** Background, edge, transition, and UI
   are one composition.
2. **One page, one emotional beat.** Curiosity, concentration, surprise, or
   celebration owns the spread.
3. **Reveal creates wonder.** Do not show the entire reward or destination at
   once.
4. **Scale creates feeling.** A tiny pet under a huge luminous tree feels like
   adventure; a close face beside one letter feels intimate and safe.
5. **Repetition creates belonging.** Recurring motifs let children predict,
   recognize, and notice change.
6. **Imperfection creates authorship.** Paper edges, brush variation, pencil
   marks, and uneven botanical forms make the world feel made, not generated.
7. **Quiet makes detail valuable.** Wonder disappears when every object glows,
   bounces, and competes.
8. **The child causes the story.** Learning visibly repairs, grows, opens, or
   awakens the world.

## What should be rejected

- English words in the visible child flow.
- Generic white dashboard cards placed over scenery.
- Progress bars and numbers as the main expression of progression.
- A map made primarily from floating circular level nodes.
- Uniformly saturated scenes with equal detail everywhere.
- Constant particles, glow, bounce, and confetti.
- Decorative animation unrelated to the current sound or action.
- Rewards that leave no lasting mark on the world.
- Identical UI containers reused for every function.
- Generic fantasy motifs with no relationship to Arabic learning, nature, or
  the child's journey.
